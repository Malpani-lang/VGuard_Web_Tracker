Own the backbone.

V-Guard doesn't have too many categories. It lets too many of them spend capital before they've earned it. This plan puts every business through one test, and uses that test to make three choices.

Score a category See how FY31 adds up
  1. Premiumise the coreWires, switchgear, fans and water heaters, sold through the electrician who picks the brand in 63% of jobs.
  2. Own the energy backboneSupply the inverter, cable and switchgear kit to every solar installer, rather than becoming one more of 20,000 installers.
  3. Make every business earn capitalA published 100-point test decides where money goes, and it applies to Sunflame as strictly as to solar.

Growth that didn't pay for itself

In FY26 V-Guard's revenue grew 7.0% to ₹5,966 crore. EBITDA grew 2.6%. Revenue was running ahead of the profit it was meant to produce.

The pattern shows up across the business. Plants ran at 59% of capacity while ₹174 crore went into new capex. Electricals grew 13.4%, mostly because copper got more expensive. Consumer Durables brought in ₹1,616 crore of revenue and ₹26 crore of profit.

A weak summer explains FY26. It doesn't explain why the same segment earned only 3.4% and 4.2% in the two years before.

The solar engine

V-Guard already makes three of the parts every rooftop installer has to buy. The Core Kit sells them as one warranted unit: string inverter, DC and AC cable, and protection and switchgear, at ₹24,000 for a 3 kW system. That's 16% of an installed system's cost, and 4.3% above a comparable imported bundle.

The gates we've written down

Each engine has a test, and the consequence of failing it is decided now, before anyone is attached to the spending. Try two of them.

Sunflame's FY28 test

Enter what Sunflame actually delivers in FY28. The decision was made in advance.

Gegadyne: exercise the option?

V-Guard holds 30.35%. The option to reach 51% is exercised only if all four gates clear and the price is under the cap.

All the gates

  • Solar kit marginsBlended gross margin of 25% or more and contribution of 18% or more. Below that, the push is capped and capital goes to premium electricals.
  • Solar returnsSegment EBIT margin above 12% and ROCE of 18% or more, after charging the capacity the existing solar business already uses. Plan: 12.1% and 28.2%.
  • Sunflame, FY28At least ₹350 crore of revenue, an 8% operating margin, positive cash and more than 15% on new capital, rising to 18% by FY29. Miss it and the business is sold.
  • GegadyneA contractual option to reach 51%, capped at a ₹1,200 crore valuation, exercised only if the technology, repeat orders, 20% residential margins and a 1,000 to 2,000 home pilot all come through.
  • Seven-day warrantyMade contractual in a district only after a pilot there hits 95% on time. Coverage grows; reliability doesn't slip.
  • Capacity, FY27Company-wide utilisation is 59%, but line-level figures aren't disclosed. Spare capacity on the inverter, cable and switchgear lines is confirmed before scale capex is released.
  • LightingScores 54. A pilot can finish; there's no money for national scale until it clears 75.
  • Home storageLaunched state by state from FY30, only where tariff spread, export rates and battery cost all clear the trigger.

Questions we expect

Why not just enter rooftop solar retail?

Over 20,000 vendors are registered under PM Surya Ghar, and the three largest organised players together hold about 50,000 installations, roughly 1% of the 50 lakh done so far. It's a crowded, low-margin business where being local matters more than the brand. Our research also found no evidence that owning V-Guard products makes people trust V-Guard for solar. Selling components to installers needs none of that trust.

What if cheaper imported inverters undercut the kit?

Chinese suppliers hold about 45.7% of string-inverter shipments. The kit is priced 4.3% above a comparable imported bundle and wins on the 18-day warranty gap, not on price. If blended gross margin falls below 25% or contribution below 18%, the push is capped and the money goes to premium electricals. The plan contribution is 21.0%, and 19.6% even if five-year warranty claims reach 13%.

Can V-Guard's service centres actually fix solar inverters?

Not today. None of the 550-plus centres is set up for grid-connected inverters, so the advantage is a network that can be converted, not one that's ready. We convert 200 by FY27, 400 by FY28 and all of them by FY29, for about ₹18 to 20 crore. The seven-day promise becomes binding in a district only once a pilot there delivers 95% on time.

Why grow at 13.2% when management is aiming for 15%?

Because the target is return, not revenue. The plan deliberately gives up about ₹400 crore of low-return revenue and doesn't chase categories that fail the test. The result is ROCE of 33.4% in FY31 against a commitment of 24%.

What if several things go wrong at once?

We tested that. Core growth falls 12% short, premium mix delivers less than half, solar reaches half its target, storage never launches, Sunflame is sold and services attachment halves. Revenue drops to ₹9,231 crore and ROCE is still 26.3%. The ₹250 crore Gegadyne money is never spent in that case, because the gates that release it aren't met.

Why is your DCF value per share below the share price?

A perpetuity DCF at an 11.7% cost of capital puts the plan at about ₹206 a share; valued at 20 times FY31 EBITDA it's about ₹396. The March 2026 price of ₹317 sits between them. We lead with the value created, ₹5,016 crore over the status quo on identical assumptions, because that isolates what the choices are worth.

What happens to Sunflame?

V-Guard paid ₹660 crore for it, and it earned about ₹13 crore in FY26. It gets one properly funded turnaround with a hard FY28 test. If it misses, it's sold and the impairment is accepted. The plan still returns 30.3% if Sunflame is removed entirely.
